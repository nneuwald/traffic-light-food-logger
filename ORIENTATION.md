# Traffic Light Food Logger: Project Orientation

A single-file web app for Traffic Light Diet food logging with clients or patients. Scan a package barcode, adjust the portion, and the app logs the food and codes it green, yellow, or red (or no colour) with the reasoning shown. Colours follow Epstein's *Food & Activity Reference Guide* (2012) exactly; see "How the app works". Built August 2026 as a working prototype; classification engine rewritten to the guide on 3 September 2026. This document is the handoff: read it first on a new machine.

## Quick start

1. Open `traffic_light_food_logger.html` in any modern browser. That's it; there is no build step, server, or install.
2. On a phone, the camera barcode scanner works best. On a laptop you can type barcode numbers or search by name instead. `verified_barcodes.csv` has 33 tested barcodes to try, spread across all three colors.
3. For real use, get a free USDA API key at https://api.data.gov/signup (takes a minute) and paste it into Settings inside the app. The bundled DEMO_KEY is limited to roughly 30 lookups per hour.

**Important:** data lives only while the page is open. Use the Backup (JSON) button to save a session and Restore to reload it. This is the prototype's biggest limitation; a production version needs real storage.

## How the app works

**Data flow for a barcode:** scan or type a code, then the app queries Open Food Facts first (broadest barcode coverage). If not found, it retries USDA FoodData Central's Branded Foods using the `gtinUpc:` field query with the code zero-padded to 14 digits. **Name search** queries both databases in parallel and merges results, USDA lab-analyzed generic foods first, each labeled with its source.

**Classification** happens entirely on-device and implements Epstein's Traffic Light Diet as written in *The Food & Activity Reference Guide* (2012), not an approximation of it. The rule is one sentence: find the food group, take the calories in one Traffic Light serving, read the colour off the group's range (guide p.6). `detectGroup()` picks the group from Open Food Facts tags, the product name and the USDA category string; `classify()` applies the range. Only vegetables can be green (≤ 30 cal per ½ cup, yellow to 45, red above). Every other group is yellow up to a cap and red above it: starchy vegetables 120, fruit 90, grains 120, cheese 83, other dairy 135, meat & seafood 158, eggs/beans/substitutes 120, nuts 68, broth soups 186, chili and bean soups 224, condiments 23. Fruit juice, dried fruit and the fats/oils/sweets group are always red, diet drinks included. Water, plain tea and coffee, and seasonings carry no colour. Cold cereal is also red above 25% of calories from sugar, the guide's one nutrient rule. Combination foods get no range; the app takes them apart instead (see below). Every verdict shows its reasons, the group can be changed in a dropdown, and any colour can be overridden.

**Combination foods (p.8).** The guide gives pizza, sandwiches, casseroles and the like no calorie range. It gives a rule: the dish is RED if it holds one full serving of a red food, and that serving "can be made up of 1 type of RED food or many RED foods". `decomposeCombo()` applies that arithmetic. Open Food Facts publishes an ingredient list with an estimated percentage for each ingredient; the app converts each percentage to grams of the label serving, colours that ingredient with `classify()` itself, and for every red component adds grams divided by that group's serving. One or more red servings makes the dish red; less than one means it is judged by its parts and comes out yellow. The reason text always shows the components and the total, because the percentages are inferred from ingredient order rather than declared.

Deciding whether a component is itself red needs its calories, which an ingredient list does not carry, so `INGREDIENT_KCAL` holds USDA values per 100 g for about a hundred ingredients that dominate US packaged dishes. `RED_SERVING_G` supplies a serving weight for the groups whose colour never depends on calories: 15 g for fats, oils and sweets, roughly a tablespoon.

The arithmetic is only run when it can be trusted. The serving has to be at least 40 g, no single ingredient may be 85% or more of the pack, at least 60% of the pack has to be priced, a headline component named on the front (cheese, pepperoni, bacon and so on) has to actually appear in the ingredient list, and an "ingredient" that merely restates the product name is rejected. Those guards exist because real records fail in exactly those ways: one "Cheese pizza" lists a flattened crust sub-recipe and no cheese at all, and a deli salmon salad lists itself as its own main ingredient. When any guard trips, the app does not guess.

**Building a dish from its parts.** Automatic decomposition only reaches packaged food with an ingredient list, which leaves out the homemade sandwich and the restaurant burrito that make up much of what a client actually eats. So a dish can be assembled by hand: press "Combination food", look each part up through the ordinary barcode, search, quick-add or custom-food paths, and press "Add as a part". The interface uses the guide's own term throughout (p.8). Every part carries its own group, colour, serving size and calories, and the number of servings is editable per row. `dishVerdict()` adds the red parts and applies the same p.8 rule, so a turkey sandwich with an ounce of cheddar and a tablespoon of mayonnaise reaches two red servings and comes out red at 441 calories, while the same sandwich without them is yellow at 231. Seeing which rows are red is the guide's own teaching point on p.8, where every tip is about components. A finished dish saves as a named recipe, so a client's usual sandwich is built once and logged with one tap after that; recipes travel in the JSON backup, which is now version 3.

`RULES.comboRedServings` holds the threshold and is editable in Settings. It defaults to 1, the guide's printed rule: "If a combination food has 1 serving of a RED food in it, then the combination food is RED." Reaching one serving is enough, which is why the comparison is "at or above" rather than "above".

**When the app cannot decide.** The verdict is a fourth state, "needs a choice", with its own dashed badge and a ◆ mark. The food card then asks the guide's own question, "Does one serving hold a full serving of a red food?", with two buttons. Until it is answered the dish counts as nothing: it is excluded from the green, yellow and red tallies and from the weekly red budget, and the day view says how many dishes are still waiting. That replaced the old behaviour, where a combination food was shown as yellow and silently escaped the two-reds-a-day limit. Of the combination foods in the saved audit draws that are still in Open Food Facts, about 70% are decided automatically; the rest reach the question, or the clinician builds them from parts.

**Serving sizes.** The guide's ranges are per household serving (½ cup vegetables, 1 oz grain or cheese, 3 oz meat, 1 cup milk, 6 oz yogurt, 1 Tbsp condiment). The app converts per-100 g data at a gram estimate for each group, stored in `DEFAULT_RULES` and editable in Settings; a few foods carry their own estimate in `SERV_OVERRIDES` (cottage cheese and sour cream ½ cup, nut butter 2 Tbsp, an egg, a pickle spear, dates as 1 medjool). Where the guide's serving is the unit as sold ("1 bar", "1 patty", the yogurt cup at 6 oz or 5.3 oz), the label's own serving weight is used when it is plausible, else the estimate. Canned fruit and applesauce are judged at the guide's ½ cup, about 125 g, so fruit packed in heavy syrup lands red as the guide prints it. The gram values were chosen so the guide's own listed foods come out the colour the guide prints. Where a product is listed in the guide, the guide wins.

**Things the guide does that surprise people, all implemented as printed.** Cottage cheese is in the Cheese subgroup at a ½-cup serving against the 83-cal cheese line, so 1% and fat-free are yellow and 2% and 4% are red (p.33, p.37). Sour cream: fat-free yellow, light and regular red (p.35, p.38). Hot dogs, deli meat and every sausage are judged at 3 oz like other meat, so a regular beef frank is red and only fat-free franks are yellow (p.44, p.52). Bacon and turkey bacon are fats, always red; Canadian bacon is meat, yellow (p.45, p.65). Dates are fresh fruit, yellow (p.21), while raisins and prunes are dried fruit, red. Lemon, lime and clam juice are cooking ingredients on the condiment page, not juices (p.73). Hummus is red (p.75), unsweetened cocoa powder is yellow (p.73). Frozen yogurt, sherbet and ice pops are sweets (p.62). Every zero-calorie sports or flavoured water (Propel, Vitaminwater Zero, Crystal Light) is red with the diet sodas (p.60), and so is anything artificially sweetened (p.2): the app reads Open Food Facts additive tags and ingredient lists for sucralose, aspartame, acesulfame, stevia and the rest, and treats artificial sweetener packets as red ingredients (p.75). Plain water, seltzer, club soda and unsweetened tea or coffee have no colour.

**Known limits of this approach.** Foods sitting a few calories from a line (banana, chicken broth, sweetened applesauce) can flip on data quality: Open Food Facts lists Swanson broth at 10 kcal/100 g, which is 25 per cup and red, where the guide lists broth at 12 per cup and yellow; it lists Mott's Original applesauce at 86 kcal/100 g, which is 107 per ½ cup and red, where the guide's generic sweetened applesauce is 84 and yellow. The colour and the number are always shown side by side so the clinician can see this. Raw leafy greens are judged at 35 g rather than 65 g because ½ cup of spinach weighs half what ½ cup of carrots does. Starchy vegetables (potato, corn, peas, beets, winter squash) are their own group and never green, exactly as the guide has them. Bakery muffins and croissants come out yellow at the 1 oz grain serving although the guide prints them red; the guide does not say what serving it used for them. Dense cold cereals (Grape-Nuts, granola, Raisin Bran without the sugar rule) are judged at 30 g like flaked cereals, where the guide uses a heavier quarter or half cup, so some come out yellow where the guide prints red. Hot cereal is a grain: oatmeal is judged at 1 oz dry or 1/2 cup cooked, and an instant packet as the packet, so flavoured instant packets at 160 calories land red as the guide prints them.

**Other structures worth knowing:** `state` holds clients and the log in memory. `foodFromOFF()` and `foodFromFDC()` normalize the two APIs into one food shape. `fdcCatsToTags()` adds Open Food Facts style tags to USDA foods; `detectGroup()` reads tags, name and USDA category together. The weekly view counts red foods against a per-client budget (default 14 per week, the guide's 2 a day) and the day view flags more than 2 on one day.

## Data sources

- **Open Food Facts** (https://world.openfoodfacts.org): community-maintained, ~4M products, no API key, CORS-open. Used for barcodes and branded search. Quality caveat: label-deep and occasionally stale; the app tells users to verify against the package.
- **USDA FoodData Central** (https://fdc.nal.usda.gov): official, free, CORS-open, needs an API key (https://api.data.gov/signup). Foundation / SR Legacy / Survey types are lab-analyzed or survey-derived generic foods; Branded is manufacturer label data. API docs: https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-guide.
- **Not yet integrated:** restaurant food. Candidates evaluated: CalorieKing (https://www.calorieking.com/us/en/developers/, strong chain coverage, commercial license, contact for pricing), Nutritionix (https://www.nutritionix.com/business/api), MenuStat (https://www.menustat.org, free snapshots). For independent restaurants, the standard practice is matching a generic USDA Survey (FNDDS) dish, which already works via name search.

## Testing

With Node.js installed, run `bash tests/extract_and_test.sh`. It re-extracts the JavaScript from the HTML and runs four suites, all offline:

- `tests_body.js`: 222 hand-written classification checks, each tied to a guide page (produce, grains, dairy, protein, drinks, condiments, the surprising cases listed above, and every category-vocabulary trap the live audit found)
- `fdc_unit.js`: 11 USDA mapping checks against `fixtures/fdc_search.json` and `fixtures/fdc_upc.json`
- `off_fixture_test.js`: 39 assertions over 33 saved Open Food Facts responses in `fixtures/off_products/`, covering colour, group and the reason text
- `fdc_generic_test.js`: 17 assertions over saved USDA generic-food responses in `fixtures/fdc_generic/`

The saved Open Food Facts fixtures were captured before `ingredients_text` and `additives_tags` were added to the request, so the artificial-sweetener rule is covered by hand-written cases only. Re-saving a fixture (for example a zero-calorie flavoured water) with `tests/probe.js` would let it be tested on real data.

The fixture suites are the important ones. Hand-written cases pass tidy tag arrays like `['en:fruits']` straight to `classify()`, so they never exercise `foodFromOFF()` or `fdcCatsToTags()` against the messy vocabulary the APIs really return. Four classification bugs lived behind exactly that gap.

`tests/probe.js` discovers new barcodes and needs internet. `tests/gen_verified_barcodes.js` rebuilds `verified_barcodes.csv`/`.json` from the saved fixtures offline; run it after any change to `classify()` or `foodFromOFF()` so the published list cannot drift.

`tests/audit_live.js` (needs internet) searches Open Food Facts for about 130 everyday US products across every food group, runs each real record through the mapper and classifier, and compares with the guide's colour and group for that kind of food. It writes `tests/audit_live_results.tsv`. On 3 September 2026 it matched 123 of 132; the 9 left were the search engine returning a different product (a Krave cereal for "tomato juice", a bare "Ore-ida" record with no tags), Open Food Facts calorie values sitting a calorie or two over a line (Ore-Ida golden crinkles 122 at 85 g against the guide's 120; Goya black beans 133 per half cup against the guide's 109), and the dense-cereal limitation below. The audit found sixteen real classifier gaps, all fixed the same day and pinned by hand-written tests: ketchup tagged as a tomato sauce went to vegetables; black beans tagged "legume-seeds" went to nuts; peanut butter tagged as a legume skipped the nut rule; canned baked beans tagged "meals" went to combination foods; tuna "in vegetable oil" and butter popcorn went to fats; Eggo waffles tagged "cakes", cheddar pretzels and Raisin Bran went to sweets, cheese and dried fruit respectively; instant oatmeal was judged as cold cereal; a condensed soup tagged "bouillon" was judged as broth; Reddi-wip tagged "desserts" and sour cream tagged only "creams" went to fats; LaCroix was red because Open Food Facts tags every essenced water "flavored-waters"; Perrier with no tags had no group; peaches in heavy syrup tagged "desserts" went to fats; Triscuit at 120.4 calories was red because the comparison did not round to whole calories the way labels do.

`tests/audit_random.js <count> <seed> [out.tsv]` draws products at random across 57 Open Food Facts categories (random page, random products on the page) and writes a TSV; `tests/audit_random_report.js` turns it into `tests/audit_random_results.md` with a hand-entered verdict per row.

**The automatic oracle.** Reading hundreds of rows by eye does not scale, so `tests/audit_analyze.js <file.tsv>` checks a draw mechanically. Every category the sampler draws from implies a small set of food groups a product from it could legitimately take: something drawn from "cheeses" may be cheese, or a sweet if it is a cheese-flavoured snack, but never a soup. Any row outside its category's set is printed for review, along with any row whose calories per 100 g fall outside a plausible band for the group the app chose. `tests/audit_reclassify.js <in.tsv> <out.tsv>` re-runs the engine over a saved draw with no network, so every past draw becomes a regression suite after each rule change.

**Where that got to.** Five draws totalling 1,700 products were run on 3 September 2026 (seeds 7, 23, 91, 305, 777 and 2024). The first 400-product draw flagged 33 mismatched groups, the next 18, then 7, then 6; after the fixes below every draw comes back with none. The failures fell into recurring shapes rather than one-off oddities, and the fixes are structural: a product the database files as a grain keeps its grain identity when a stray word from another group appears in its name (a cinnamon raisin bagel is not dried fruit, Cape Cod popcorn is not cod, basmati rice with bell peppers is not a vegetable); a product filed as meat, fish or a meat substitute keeps that identity through sauce, oil and drink words (sardines in tomato sauce, tuna with olive oil, beer bratwurst, baked tofu teriyaki); a seasoning has to be a jar of seasoning rather than any food whose name contains "salt" or "herbs", which had been turning canned tuna and chili oil into no-colour foods; and a drink that matches nothing else now falls to Fats, Oils, Sweets & Others rather than to no group at all. Smaller corrections: pickles, hummus, dressings and canned coconut milk reach the condiment group instead of nuts, dairy or fats; gnocchi, ravioli, tostadas, bean-thread noodles and rice sticks reach grains; wax beans and shredded coleslaw reach vegetables; lima beans reach protein; and calories are rounded to whole numbers before comparison because both the guide and food labels are stated that way. Two draws on 3 September 2026 (seeds 7 and 23) found nine more traps, all fixed and tested: hamburger and hot dog buns read as burgers; plain rice tagged "meals" read as a combination food; chili-seasoned tuna read as chili; an ice cream sandwich read as a sandwich; a salad kit with pecans read as nuts; frozen chopped onions tagged "condiments" read as a condiment; street-taco tortillas read as tacos; whole pitted dates tagged "dried fruit" went red where the guide prints dates yellow; tortilla chips went to grains instead of salty snacks; canned coconut milk went to fats instead of ingredients. On the final seed-23 draw, 92 of 100 follow the guide's rule for the food and the other 8 are the rule applied correctly to doubtful database values (a 406 kcal/100 g corn flakes, a 329 kcal/100 g veggie burger, dehydrated hash browns and condensed soup judged per dry or condensed weight, raw chicken drumsticks judged at raw weight).

If you change classification rules, add a matching test case, and prefer a real saved API response over a hand-written one.

Past bugs worth remembering, all the same shape: **matching category text by substring instead of by whole word.**

- Open Food Facts files yogurts under "desserts", so plain Greek yogurt came out red
- The word "chocolate" contains "cola", so chocolate bars went through the soda detector
- `en:plant-based-foods-and-beverages` contains "beverages", and OFF puts it on nearly every plant food, so bread, peas, oats and pasta were all judged as drinks. Canned peas came out red
- `en:unsweetened-beverages` contains "sweetened-beverages", so bottled water was read as soda and came out red
- OFF files cereal grains under `en:seeds`, so rice and oats claimed the nut exemption from the fat and calorie-density rules

Two related non-substring bugs: the diet-drink escape required `sugars_100g` to be present, but OFF omits it on water and diet soda, so both fell through to red. And USDA Survey (FNDDS) records set `foodCategory` to the food's own WWEIA name ("Apples", "Carrots") rather than the "Fruits and Fruit Juices" wording Foundation and SR Legacy use, so generic produce got no tag and fell through to yellow.

All have regression tests. `hasCat()` now matches whole hyphen-delimited words, and `tagsLookLikeBeverage()` explicitly rejects the `*-foods-and-beverages` umbrella tags.

## Known limitations and roadmap ideas

No persistence beyond JSON backup files (the top priority for a production version). The single-file design exposes any API key in Settings to whoever has the file; fine for internal use, but a paid data source like CalorieKing would need a small server in the middle. Restaurant food coverage is thin pending a dedicated source. A real mobile app (React Native or similar) would add proper storage, accounts, camera reliability, and offline caching. Style note: no em dashes in app text.
