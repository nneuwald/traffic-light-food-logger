# Traffic Light Food Logger: Project Orientation

A single-file web app for Traffic Light Diet food logging with clients or patients. Scan a package barcode, adjust the portion, and the app logs the food and codes it green, yellow, or red (or no colour) with the reasoning shown. Colours follow Epstein's *Food & Activity Reference Guide* (2012) exactly; see "How the app works". Built August 2026 as a working prototype; classification engine rewritten to the guide on 3 September 2026. This document is the handoff: read it first on a new machine.

## Quick start

1. Open `traffic_light_food_logger.html` in any modern browser. That's it; there is no build step, server, or install.
2. On a phone, the camera barcode scanner works best. On a laptop you can type barcode numbers or search by name instead.
3. For real use, get a free USDA API key at https://api.data.gov/signup (takes a minute) and paste it into Settings inside the app. The key is remembered on that device. The bundled DEMO_KEY allows only about 10 searches an hour per network (measured September 2026), so a clinic will run it out in minutes.

**Important:** data lives only while the page is open. Use the Backup (JSON) button to save a session and Restore to reload it. This is the prototype's biggest limitation; a production version needs real storage.

## How the app works

**Data flow for a barcode:** scan or type a code, then the app queries USDA FoodData Central's Branded Foods using the `gtinUpc:` field query with the code zero-padded to 14 digits, and to 13 if that misses. A miss is an ordinary outcome, since USDA Branded holds manufacturer submissions rather than every package on a US shelf, so the hint points at name search and manual entry instead of reporting a fault. **Name search** checks the built-in quick-add list, then makes one USDA request across Foundation, SR Legacy, Survey (FNDDS) and Branded, splits the answer so generic records lead, and ranks each list with `rankByName()` before showing it, because USDA's own relevance order buries the plain record under elaborate ones.

**Classification** happens entirely on-device and implements Epstein's Traffic Light Diet as written in *The Food & Activity Reference Guide* (2012), not an approximation of it. The rule is one sentence: find the food group, take the calories in one Traffic Light serving, read the colour off the group's range (guide p.6). `detectGroup()` picks the group from the classifier's category tags, the product name and the USDA category string; `classify()` applies the range. Only vegetables can be green (≤ 30 cal per ½ cup, yellow to 45, red above). Every other group is yellow up to a cap and red above it: starchy vegetables 120, fruit 90, grains 120, cheese 83, other dairy 135, meat & seafood 158, eggs/beans/substitutes 120, nuts 68, broth soups 186, chili and bean soups 224, condiments 23. Fruit juice, dried fruit and the fats/oils/sweets group are always red, diet drinks included. Water, plain tea and coffee, and seasonings carry no colour. Cold cereal is also red above 25% of calories from sugar, the guide's one nutrient rule. Combination foods get no range; the app takes them apart instead (see below). Every verdict shows its reasons, the group can be changed in a dropdown, and any colour can be overridden.

**Combination foods (p.8).** The guide gives pizza, sandwiches, casseroles and the like no calorie range. It gives a rule: the dish is RED if it holds one full serving of a red food, and that serving "can be made up of 1 type of RED food or many RED foods". `decomposeCombo()` applies that arithmetic to a food record carrying a percentage per ingredient. USDA publishes none, so `ingredientsFromFDC()` estimates them from the order the ingredients are listed in before the record reaches the engine (see the next section). The app converts each percentage to grams of the label serving, colours that ingredient with `classify()` itself, and for every red component adds grams divided by that group's serving. One or more red servings makes the dish red; less than one means it is judged by its parts and comes out yellow. The reason text always shows the components and the total, because the percentages are inferred from ingredient order rather than declared.

Deciding whether a component is itself red needs its calories, which an ingredient list does not carry, so `INGREDIENT_KCAL` holds USDA values per 100 g for about a hundred ingredients that dominate US packaged dishes. `RED_SERVING_G` supplies a serving weight for the groups whose colour never depends on calories: 15 g for fats, oils and sweets, roughly a tablespoon.

The arithmetic is only run when it can be trusted. The serving has to be at least 40 g, no single ingredient may be 85% or more of the pack, at least 60% of the pack has to be priced, a headline component named on the front (cheese, pepperoni, bacon and so on) has to actually appear in the ingredient list, and an "ingredient" that merely restates the product name is rejected. Those guards exist because real records fail in exactly those ways: one "Cheese pizza" lists a flattened crust sub-recipe and no cheese at all, and a deli salmon salad lists itself as its own main ingredient. When any guard trips, the app does not guess.

**Estimating the percentages (18 September 2026).** USDA publishes an ingredient list as one plain-text string with no percentage against anything, so when Open Food Facts was removed the decomposition above had nothing to work on and every mixed dish fell through to the clinician's question. Open Food Facts was not reading percentages off labels either, though, because labels do not carry them: it computed them, from the one thing every US label does guarantee, that ingredients are listed in descending order by weight (21 CFR 101.4). The inference was what went missing, not the data, so the app now does it.

`parseUsdaIngredients()` splits the string on top-level commas only, so `CRUST (ENRICHED FLOUR, WATER), TOMATO SAUCE` is two ingredients and not four; it keeps the head noun and drops the parenthetical composition, since `INGREDIENT_KCAL` prices "crust" and "enriched flour" alike. A `CONTAINS 2% OR LESS OF:` marker is a declared bound rather than noise, so everything after it is capped at 2%. An allergen line, a "may contain", or a facility note ends the recipe and is cut, because all three only ever appear at the end.

`estimateIngredientPercents()` then gives share *i* of *n* a weight proportional to *i* to the power `-INGREDIENT_DECAY`, normalised to 100. The exponent is measured rather than chosen. `tests/audit_combo_estimate.js` draws real combination foods that publish both their ingredient text and a percentage per ingredient, classifies each twice, and counts how often the estimate lands on the same colour. Agreement rises from 62% at a flat exponent of 0 to a broad plateau between 1.5 and 2 and falls away above it; **1.75 matched 96 of 140 products and agreed on 83% of the 116 it was willing to decide**, against 72% for the order-statistic curve tried first. (That audit and its cached draw were retired on 9 October 2026 along with the other Open Food Facts tooling, since USDA publishes no percentages to calibrate against; they are in the git history.) The draw was cached in `tests/audit_combo_estimate_products.json` so the exponent can be re-swept offline after a rule change.

Two things are worth keeping in view. The estimate is an estimate, always shown with its arithmetic and overridable, and the reason text says so in as many words. And it is not the only gate: the guards above still apply, which is why 24 of those 140 dishes were refused outright rather than decided on a weak reading. A single lasagna record suggested early on that a much flatter curve was right, because the source gave its top five ingredients nearly equal shares; the sweep across 140 products said otherwise, and verdicts are what the number is for. A colour turns on whether one red ingredient reaches a full serving, and a flat curve pushes mid-list cheese and meat over that line in dishes the source itself calls yellow.

**Building a dish from its parts.** Automatic decomposition only reaches packaged food with an ingredient list, which leaves out the homemade sandwich and the restaurant burrito that make up much of what a client actually eats. So a dish can be assembled by hand: press "Combination food", look each part up through the ordinary barcode, search, quick-add or custom-food paths, and press "Add as a part". The interface uses the guide's own term throughout (p.8). Every part carries its own group, colour, serving size and calories, and the number of servings is editable per row. `dishVerdict()` adds the red parts and applies the same p.8 rule, so a turkey sandwich with an ounce of cheddar and a tablespoon of mayonnaise reaches two red servings and comes out red at 441 calories, while the same sandwich without them is yellow at 231. Seeing which rows are red is the guide's own teaching point on p.8, where every tip is about components. A finished dish saves as a named recipe, so a client's usual sandwich is built once and logged with one tap after that; recipes travel in the JSON backup, which is now version 3.

`RULES.comboRedServings` holds the threshold and is editable in Settings. It defaults to 1, the guide's printed rule: "If a combination food has 1 serving of a RED food in it, then the combination food is RED." Reaching one serving is enough, which is why the comparison is "at or above" rather than "above".

**When the app cannot decide.** The verdict is a fourth state, "needs a choice", with its own dashed badge and a ◆ mark. The food card then asks the guide's own question, "Does one serving hold a full serving of a red food?", with two buttons. Until it is answered the dish counts as nothing: it is excluded from the green, yellow and red tallies and from the weekly red budget, and the day view says how many dishes are still waiting. That replaced the old behaviour, where a combination food was shown as yellow and silently escaped the two-reds-a-day limit. Every combination food now reaches that question or is built from parts, because USDA carries no ingredient percentages. On the saved audit draws, whose records did carry them, about 70% were decided automatically, which is the coverage that returns if a source with percentages is added.

**Serving sizes.** The guide's ranges are per household serving (½ cup vegetables, 1 oz grain or cheese, 3 oz meat, 1 cup milk, 6 oz yogurt, 1 Tbsp condiment). The app converts per-100 g data at a gram estimate for each group, stored in `DEFAULT_RULES` and editable in Settings; a few foods carry their own estimate in `SERV_OVERRIDES` (cottage cheese and sour cream ½ cup, nut butter 2 Tbsp, an egg, a pickle spear, dates as 1 medjool). Where the guide's serving is the unit as sold ("1 bar", "1 patty", the yogurt cup at 6 oz or 5.3 oz), the label's own serving weight is used when it is plausible, else the estimate. Canned fruit and applesauce are judged at the guide's ½ cup, about 125 g, so fruit packed in heavy syrup lands red as the guide prints it. The gram values were chosen so the guide's own listed foods come out the colour the guide prints. Where a product is listed in the guide, the guide wins.

**Portions and servings counted (11 September 2026).** The colour is fixed per FRG serving, but how much was eaten is a second question, and the guide's serving is often not the label's: a plain 4" bagel is ¼ bagel in the guide (p.28) and a whole bagel on the package, while the daily limit is worded in servings, "Eat 2 or less servings of RED foods per day" (p.3). The portion buttons therefore multiply the label serving, which is what the client is holding, and `portionBasis()`, `frgServings()` and `portionLine()` convert that to FRG servings for the food card, the log line and the counts, so a whole bagel reads "1 label serving, 1 bagel (105 g) = about 3.8 FRG servings". A weight in grams can be typed instead of pressing a button. Each entry stores `servings` and `grams`; the day tiles, the week chart and the red budget add up servings, and entries from before this change count as one each. A red combination food counts its red content, since those are literally servings of red foods eaten; a yellow one counts none. When a product record has no label serving, which is common on USDA generic foods, the card opens at the guide's serving for the group rather than at 100 g: cream cheese at 1 oz and about 90 calories instead of 100 g and 321. Water and an undecided combination food, which have no group serving, still fall back to 100 g.

**Things the guide does that surprise people, all implemented as printed.** Cottage cheese is in the Cheese subgroup at a ½-cup serving against the 83-cal cheese line, so 1% and fat-free are yellow and 2% and 4% are red (p.33, p.37). Sour cream: fat-free yellow, light and regular red (p.35, p.38). Hot dogs, deli meat and every sausage are judged at 3 oz like other meat, so a regular beef frank is red and only fat-free franks are yellow (p.44, p.52). Bacon and turkey bacon are fats, always red; Canadian bacon is meat, yellow (p.45, p.65). Dates are fresh fruit, yellow (p.21), while raisins and prunes are dried fruit, red. Lemon, lime and clam juice are cooking ingredients on the condiment page, not juices (p.73). Hummus is red (p.75), unsweetened cocoa powder is yellow (p.73). Frozen yogurt, sherbet and ice pops are sweets (p.62). Every zero-calorie sports or flavoured water (Propel, Vitaminwater Zero, Crystal Light) is red with the diet sodas (p.60), and so is anything artificially sweetened (p.2): the app reads the USDA ingredient list for sucralose, aspartame, acesulfame, stevia and the rest, and treats artificial sweetener packets as red ingredients (p.75). Plain water, seltzer, club soda and unsweetened tea or coffee have no colour.

**Known limits of this approach.** Foods sitting a few calories from a line (banana, chicken broth, sweetened applesauce) can flip on data quality: one branded record lists Swanson broth at 10 kcal/100 g, which is 25 per cup and red, where the guide lists broth at 12 per cup and yellow; it lists Mott's Original applesauce at 86 kcal/100 g, which is 107 per ½ cup and red, where the guide's generic sweetened applesauce is 84 and yellow. The colour and the number are always shown side by side so the clinician can see this. Raw leafy greens are judged at 35 g rather than 65 g because ½ cup of spinach weighs half what ½ cup of carrots does. Starchy vegetables (potato, corn, peas, beets, winter squash) are their own group and never green, exactly as the guide has them. Bakery muffins and croissants come out yellow at the 1 oz grain serving although the guide prints them red; the guide does not say what serving it used for them. Dense cold cereals (Grape-Nuts, granola, Raisin Bran without the sugar rule) are judged at 30 g like flaked cereals, where the guide uses a heavier quarter or half cup, so some come out yellow where the guide prints red. Hot cereal is a grain: oatmeal is judged at 1 oz dry or 1/2 cup cooked, and an instant packet as the packet, so flavoured instant packets at 160 calories land red as the guide prints them.

**Name search (superseded on 18 September 2026, when the app stopped querying Open Food Facts).** Open Food Facts rate-limits its search endpoint to a handful of requests a minute, and a throttled request reaches the browser as a plain network error rather than a status code. `offSearch()` used to treat one failure as fatal and fall through to search.openfoodfacts.org, which sends no CORS header and so can never load in a browser; the real cause was masked and the clinician was told the databases were unreachable. It now retries the endpoint that works, three times with a short backoff. If a search still comes back empty, `queryVariants()` retries up to two spellings of the last word, so "wheat thin" finds Wheat Thins and "berry" finds berries; the hint says which spelling produced the results. The messages now separate "busy, try again" from "no matches", and say to add a personal USDA key when the shared DEMO_KEY has been rate-limited, which is what a USDA 429 means.

**Name search, second round (15 September 2026).** A tester's screenshots showed "oatmeal" returning French oat cookies and "skim milk" returning Nutella, with USDA reported unavailable in one and rate-limited in the other. Both messages had one cause. USDA now fronts `/foods/search` with a proxy (`X-Nginx-Intercept: portal-foods-search`) that answers 400 to a GET whose `dataType` list holds a value with a space, "SR Legacy" or "Survey (FNDDS)", however it is encoded; a single value or a list without spaces still passes, so barcode lookups kept working while every name search failed. The request string had not changed since the first commit, so this changed on USDA's side. The failed calls still counted against the demo key, whose limit header now reads 10 an hour per IP address rather than the 30 the docs state, so ten searches later the 400s became 429s. `fdcSearch()` now sends the documented POST with a JSON body, which the proxy accepts and whose CORS preflight is answered. With USDA gone, all a searcher saw was Open Food Facts' text search on the worldwide site with no sort order, which matches words anywhere in a record, ingredients included, and returns the most recently edited. `offSearchOnce()` now asks the US site sorted by scan count, which returned Quaker Instant Oatmeal, Oatmeal Squares, Better Oats and Millville for "oatmeal", and `rankByName()` lifts records whose name holds every search word above ingredient-only matches. The search box also matches the built-in quick-add list through `quickMatches()`, so skim milk is found with no network at all. The USDA key is now kept in `localStorage` by `loadPrefs()` and `savePrefs()`, the one thing the prototype persists, because every fresh visit to the GitHub Pages link otherwise ran on the shared demo key. Open Food Facts also began answering anonymous search with a 503 "temporarily unavailable" page after about five requests in a few minutes, lasting several minutes; the app's three quick retries cannot outwait that, and the hint says the branded products are missing. `search.openfoodfacts.org` still sends no `Access-Control-Allow-Origin`, so it remains unusable from a browser. `tests/search_test.js` pins the request shapes and the ranking.

**USDA only (18 September 2026).** The tracker being built from this prototype is to pull exclusively from the USDA database, so the app now does too. Open Food Facts is gone: `offByBarcode()`, `offSearchOnce()`, `offSearch()`, `OFF_FIELDS` and `foodFromOFF()` were deleted, and the app makes exactly one food-data request, to `api.nal.usda.gov`. Nothing in the classification engine changed, which is the point worth carrying into the other build: `classify()` never touched the network, so removing a data source was a change to the lookup layer alone and all 237 hand-written rule checks passed untouched.

Three consequences are worth knowing before this is treated as a like-for-like swap. **Barcode coverage drops**, because Open Food Facts was carrying packages USDA Branded does not have; of the first five entries in `verified_barcodes.csv`, only Hunt's diced tomatoes and Ben's Original brown rice resolved on USDA alone, with Grimmway baby carrots, Del Monte green beans and Barilla spaghetti missing, before the shared demo key rate-limited the check. Re-run that with a real key before relying on a number. **Automatic decomposition of mixed dishes had to be rebuilt**, because USDA publishes ingredient lists as plain text and the arithmetic needs a percentage per ingredient. It now estimates them from list order; see the next section. **Name ranking had to move**: `rankByName()` used to tidy the Open Food Facts results, and USDA's own relevance order puts elaborate records above plain ones ("Milk, whole" under "Milk, chocolate, whole"), so `lookup()` now ranks the generic and branded lists itself.

(Superseded on 9 October 2026: see "Purely USDA" below.) The category tags the engine reads (`en:fruits`, `en:skimmed-milks`) are Open Food Facts' vocabulary and stay, now as an internal interface that `fdcCatsToTags()` translates USDA categories into. That is what keeps the engine source-agnostic, and it is why `fixtures/off_products/` is still worth keeping: those 33 records were tagged by contributors rather than produced by our own mapper, so they are the one sample the engine cannot quietly agree with itself about. The mapper they need now lives in `tests/off_fixture_adapter.js`, clearly marked as test scaffolding rather than application code.

**Seven bugs USDA's house style exposed (18 September 2026).** The group rules were written against Open Food Facts product names. With Open Food Facts removed, USDA's descriptions became the only text the rules ever see, and USDA writes them very differently: it inverts the name ("Beans, snap, green"), spells out colour variants ("Grapes, red or green, raw"), calls dried legumes "mature seeds", files rice and pasta under "Cereal Grains and Pasta", and writes hot oatmeal as "Cereals, oats, regular and quick, cooked with water". None of this is a new fault; the rules simply met this vocabulary for the first time as the sole source. Sixty everyday foods found five of these, and widening to 155 found two more:

| Food | Was | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Grapes, red or green, raw | vegetable, **GREEN** | `FDC_VEG_WORDS` and `LEAFY_WORDS` both hold "greens", and plural tolerance makes "green" match it. A name-word match alone now loses to a fruit tag |
| Beans, snap, green, canned | protein | the phrase rule wanted "green beans"; it now also accepts bean plus snap, string or wax |
| Beans, black, mature seeds | nuts | "seeds" reached the nut rule; `legumey` now recognises "mature seeds" and USDA's inverted bean names |
| Rice and pasta, cooked | cold cereal | `fdcCatsToTags()` matched "cereal" loosely, which also brought the 25% sugar rule and a 30 g serving |
| Tortillas, ready-to-bake or -fry | starchy vegetable | "fry" plural-matches "fries"; a grain-shaped product is now never fried potatoes |
| Cereals, oats, cooked with water | cold cereal | the leading "Cereals" won. Hot cereal now also keys on "oats", excluding anything ready-to-eat, tagged as a breakfast cereal, or a drink |
| Beverages, carbonated, club soda | sweets, **RED** | `detectGroup()` excluded club soda by name, but the mapper had already tagged it `en:sodas`, and tags are read first |

Grapes coming out green was the worst of them, because green is the one colour that tells a client a food is unlimited. Two of the fixes were caught overreaching by the existing suites rather than by inspection: keying hot cereal on "oats" first sent Honey Nut Cheerios to grains, which `off_fixture_test.js` failed, and then sent oat milk to grains, which the 1700-product audit failed. Both are now narrowed. `tests/audit_usda_offline.js` is the gate that holds all of this, and it fails the test run rather than printing a warning.

**Purely USDA (9 October 2026).** The app had stopped fetching from Open Food Facts on 18 September, but its classifier still carried that source's logic: 61 category-tag checks in `detectGroup()`, of which 41 named tags only Open Food Facts ever produced. Those could never fire on a USDA record, and a developer reading the file could not have told which rules were live. They are gone, along with the filter for Open Food Facts' umbrella tags, the E-number sweetener check (USDA has no additives field), an unused drink-tag helper, and the `en:` prefix on the tags themselves. The built-in quick-add foods now carry a USDA category and go through the same mapper as a searched record. Every tag `detectGroup()` still checks is one `fdcCatsToTags()` can produce, and the file no longer names Open Food Facts anywhere.

Removing dead code should change nothing, and that was checked rather than assumed: `tests/characterize.js` records what the engine does for 200 USDA-shaped foods at five calorie levels each, 1,000 cases, before anything was cut, and fails on any difference afterwards. Every edit to the logic was verified against it.

Doing it properly meant asking what those Open Food Facts rules had been for, and several were doing real work that nothing on the USDA side replaced. Converting the hand-written suite to describe its foods as USDA does, a name plus a USDA category, put 36 of its 221 cases on red. Most traced to guards that had leaned on Open Food Facts tags, now rebuilt on the information USDA does carry:

- **Meat and fish.** The guard that keeps a meat in its group through a sauce word only ever fired on Open Food Facts tags, so on USDA data sardines in tomato sauce came out a vegetable; teriyaki chicken, pulled pork in BBQ sauce and turkey with gravy came out condiments; and beer bratwurst came out a sweet. It now reads USDA's own categories (Finfish and Shellfish, Poultry, Beef, Pork, Sausages and Luncheon Meats). Turkey with gravy also turned up a second fault: the gravy word was shrinking a meat's serving to the 60 g gravy serving, and meat now stays at 3 oz.
- **USDA's category shortcuts.** USDA files salad dressings under Fats and Oils and cocoa powder under Sweets, and the shortcut that makes those categories always red was taking them, though the guide makes both condiments (p.73, p.75). Ranch is red either way; a fat-free dressing should be a yellow condiment and was not. USDA's Sweets category also tagged honey and preserves as candy, which blocked the rule that files them as red ingredients at 1 Tbsp.
- **Canned fruit "in juice".** The mapper read "Pineapple, canned, juice pack" and "in 100% juice" as fruit juice, which is always red. The pack liquid is now recognised as pack liquid.
- **Smaller ones.** A drink with a fruit word in its name ("lemon-lime thirst quencher") fell through to the fruit group; hot dog buns went to meat, and "Bun size franks" then went to grain; an egg carton named "...Cage Free Chickens" went to meat; a gumbo vegetable mix became a soup; carrot "chips" became a snack; a chickpea dish USDA files as a meal escaped the combination-food rule; "Oatmeal, cooked with water" was read as water; and hot oatmeal, which USDA files under Breakfast Cereals beside the ready-to-eat kind, was being judged as cold cereal.

The remaining failures were cases filed under the wrong USDA category during the conversion, now corrected to USDA's real filing, and two cases whose premise was an Open Food Facts quirk: a Romanian product name, restated as "Prune cake", and a mis-tagged energy drink, retitled by the behaviour it pins. Twenty-one case titles that named an Open Food Facts tag were retitled by the behaviour they pin.

**Other structures worth knowing:** `state` holds clients and the log in memory. `foodFromFDC()` normalizes a USDA record into the one food shape the rest of the app uses. `fdcCatsToTags()` derives a short list of category tags (`fruits`, `skimmed-milks`, twenty in all) from USDA's category and description; `detectGroup()` reads those tags, the product name and the USDA category string together, and every tag it checks for is one the mapper can produce. The weekly view adds up red servings against a per-client budget (default 14 per week, the guide's 2 servings a day) and the day view flags more than 2 on one day.

## Data sources

- **USDA FoodData Central** (https://fdc.nal.usda.gov): official, free, CORS-open, needs an API key (https://api.data.gov/signup). Foundation / SR Legacy / Survey types are lab-analyzed or survey-derived generic foods; Branded is manufacturer label data. API docs: https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-guide.
- **Not yet integrated:** restaurant food. Candidates evaluated: CalorieKing (https://www.calorieking.com/us/en/developers/, strong chain coverage, commercial license, contact for pricing), Nutritionix (https://www.nutritionix.com/business/api), MenuStat (https://www.menustat.org, free snapshots). For independent restaurants, the standard practice is matching a generic USDA Survey (FNDDS) dish, which already works via name search.

## Testing

With Node.js installed, run `bash tests/extract_and_test.sh`. It re-extracts the JavaScript from the HTML and runs every suite offline, and the run fails if any of them does:

- `tests_body.js`: 221 hand-written classification cases, each tied to a guide page. Every food is described as USDA describes it, a name and a USDA category, and goes through `fdcCatsToTags()` before it is classified, so the cases exercise the path the app really uses. Categories follow USDA's own filing, quirks included: peanut butter, hummus and tofu are Legumes; salad dressings are Fats and Oils; hot cereal is Breakfast Cereals.
- `fdc_unit.js`: 11 USDA mapping checks against `fixtures/fdc_search.json` and `fixtures/fdc_upc.json`
- `fdc_generic_test.js`: 17 assertions over saved USDA generic-food responses in `fixtures/fdc_generic/`
- `search_test.js`: 23 checks of the name-search plumbing: the USDA request is a POST with a JSON body, name matches rank first, and the built-in quick-add list is searched
- `lookup_test.js`: 15 checks of the lookup flow against a stubbed USDA
- `combo_estimate_test.js`: 23 checks of the ingredient-list reading behind combination foods
- `audit_usda_offline.js`: 160 everyday foods written as USDA returns them, carried through `foodFromFDC()` and `classify()` together and checked against the guide
- `characterize.js`: the refactoring net. It records what the engine does today for 200 USDA-shaped foods at five calorie levels, 1,000 cases, and fails on any change. It makes no claim about correctness; the suites above do that. When a change is meant to move results, read the diff it prints, and if every line is intended, re-save with `node tests/characterize.js --save`.

`tests/audit_usda_foods.js` runs the same everyday-foods list against the live USDA search, which the offline suites cannot reach. It needs internet and, in practice, your own key: `FDC_KEY=your-key node tests/audit_usda_foods.js`. It caches as it goes, so the demo key's hourly limit does not lose progress.

If you change a classification rule, add a matching case to `tests_body.js`, describe the food the way USDA would, and run `characterize.js` to see what else the change moved.

**Before USDA-only.** Until October 2026 the classifier was also audited against Open Food Facts: a live audit of about 130 named products, five random draws totalling 1,700 products, and 33 saved product records. Those audits found most of the traps this classifier now guards against, and every one of their findings was pinned as a hand-written case, which is where that coverage lives now. The tooling and saved draws were retired with the source, because they could only ever test input the app can no longer receive; they remain in the git history.

Past bugs worth remembering, all the same shape: **matching text by substring instead of by whole word.** The word "chocolate" contains "cola", so chocolate bars went through the soda detector; a tag ending "-foods-and-beverages" contained "beverages", so bread, peas and pasta were judged as drinks; "unsweetened-beverages" contained "sweetened-beverages", so bottled water came out red. `hasCat()` now matches whole hyphen-delimited words. USDA Survey (FNDDS) records also set `foodCategory` to the food's own WWEIA name ("Apples", "Carrots") rather than the "Fruits and Fruit Juices" wording Foundation and SR Legacy use, so generic produce is recognised by name as well as by category.

## Known limitations and roadmap ideas

No persistence beyond JSON backup files, apart from the USDA key (the top priority for a production version). The single-file design exposes any API key in Settings to whoever has the file; fine for internal use, but a paid data source like CalorieKing would need a small server in the middle. Restaurant food coverage is thin pending a dedicated source. A real mobile app (React Native or similar) would add proper storage, accounts, camera reliability, and offline caching. Style note: no em dashes in app text.

## Status and next steps (3 September 2026)

**Done.** The classification engine implements the Food Reference Guide rule (food group, then calories per Traffic Light serving, then the group's range) with every threshold from the guide's page 6. Food group is auto-detected and editable per item. Four test suites pass offline. `verified_barcodes.csv` lists 33 real barcodes with their guide colour and group.

**Audited against the guide itself, 3 September 2026 (afternoon).** The page-6 table matches the app exactly. Reading the food listings turned up these differences, all fixed the same day: hot dogs were judged at 50 g instead of the guide's 3 oz (regular franks came out yellow); dates were filed as dried fruit (red) though the guide lists them as fresh fruit (yellow); sour cream was judged at 2 Tbsp so regular sour cream came out yellow where the guide prints red; Canadian bacon went red with bacon; lemon and lime juice went red as juices; unsweetened iced tea went red as a soft drink; zero-calorie flavoured waters and artificial-sweetener packets came out "no colour" or yellow where the guide has them red; canned fruit in heavy syrup came out yellow because the fruit serving was 90 g rather than the guide's ½ cup; granola bars and yogurt cups were judged at a fixed weight rather than the unit sold. One published colour changed: Mott's Original applesauce is now red (see "Known limits").

**Judgment calls made in the rewrite, easy to revisit in Settings.** Serving gram estimates per group (vegetables 65 g, raw leafy greens 35 g, fruit 90 g fresh or 125 g canned, grains 28 g dry or 75 g cooked, cheese 28 g, dairy 245 g, yogurt 170 g or the cup sold, meat 85 g, beans 90 g plain or 127 g in sauce, nuts 28 g, soups 245 g, condiments 15 g). The bean figure was corrected from 125 g on 3 September 2026: working backwards from the guide's own entries, half a cup of drained beans weighs about 86 to 99 g, and the heavier estimate had been pushing ordinary canned beans over the 120-calorie line into red. Sour cream is judged at ½ cup because that is the only serving that reproduces the guide's three printed colours; the guide's own serving column for it is unreadable in the PDF.
